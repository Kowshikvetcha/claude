## Warm-up

### Q1. What is RAG, and why would you reach for it instead of just fine-tuning the LLM on your documents?
**Answer.** RAG retrieves relevant passages at query time and stuffs them into the prompt, letting the model answer grounded in that evidence rather than relying purely on parametric memory. Fine-tuning bakes knowledge into weights, which is expensive to update (any document change needs retraining), doesn't give you a citable source for an answer, and is a poor fit for knowledge that changes often or is too voluminous to fit in training data economically. RAG gives you near-real-time freshness (re-index a new document, it's queryable immediately), traceability (you can point to the exact passage used), and lets you scope access per-query (a user only retrieves documents they're authorized to see) — none of which fine-tuning gives you for free.
**Follow-ups.** When would fine-tuning actually be the better call over RAG? → When you need to change the model's *behavior/style/format* rather than inject facts (e.g. always respond in a specific JSON schema, adopt a domain-specific reasoning pattern) — that's a distributional shift in how the model responds, which retrieval alone can't produce.

### Q2. Where does a real-world RAG pipeline most often fail first, before you even get to retrieval quality?
**Answer.** Document ingestion and parsing — turning a messy real-world PDF, scanned image, or HTML page into clean, structured text. Tables get flattened into unreadable text soup, multi-column layouts get read in the wrong order, headers/footers pollute every chunk, and scanned documents need OCR that introduces its own error rate. If ingestion silently mangles the source, no amount of downstream chunking or embedding tuning recovers the lost information — garbage in, garbage retrieved.
**Follow-ups.** How do you catch a silent ingestion failure before it reaches production retrieval? → Spot-check parsed output against the source document on a sample, track parse-failure/low-confidence-OCR rates as a pipeline metric, and specifically test on your worst document types (scanned forms, dense tables) rather than only clean text PDFs.
**Page.** [[document-ingestion-and-parsing]]

### Q3. Fixed-size chunking versus semantic/structure-aware chunking — what's the actual tradeoff?
**Answer.** Fixed-size chunking (e.g. 512 tokens with overlap) is cheap, predictable, and works acceptably on homogeneous prose, but it cuts through sentences, tables, and logical sections arbitrarily, sometimes splitting a claim from the evidence that supports it across two chunks that get retrieved (or not) independently. Semantic/structure-aware chunking (split on headings, paragraphs, or via embedding-similarity boundaries) preserves coherent units, improving retrieval precision, at the cost of more preprocessing complexity and variable chunk sizes that complicate downstream context-budget planning. In practice, structure-aware chunking that respects document boundaries (a table stays one chunk, a section stays together) beats naive fixed-size splitting on almost any real corpus.
**Follow-ups.** What's chunk overlap for, and what's the failure mode of setting it too high or too low? → Overlap preserves context that would otherwise be cut at a chunk boundary; too little overlap loses cross-boundary context (a sentence split mid-thought), too much overlap bloats the index with near-duplicate content that wastes retrieval slots on redundant information.
**Page.** [[chunking-strategies]]

### Q4. How do you choose and evaluate an embedding model for retrieval, rather than just picking whatever's popular?
**Answer.** Evaluate on your own domain, not generic benchmarks (MTEB leaderboard rank doesn't guarantee it's good on your legal/medical/code corpus) — build a small labeled retrieval eval set (query, relevant-passage pairs) from your actual data and measure recall@k directly. Key tradeoffs: embedding dimensionality (higher dimension often better recall but more storage/compute), context length the model was trained for (a model trained on short sentences degrades on long chunks), whether it's asymmetric (separate query/passage encoders, better for question-to-passage retrieval) versus symmetric, and whether you need multilingual support. Also check licensing and whether it's served (API cost/latency per call) or self-hosted (infra cost but no per-call fee and full data control).
**Follow-ups.** Why can a higher-MTEB-ranked embedding model perform worse on your specific corpus? → MTEB aggregates across many generic tasks/domains; a model can be strong on average while being weak on your corpus's specific vocabulary (e.g. dense technical jargon or code) that's underrepresented in its training mix — domain-specific evaluation is the only way to know.

## Core

### Q5. How do you choose a vector database / index for production retrieval at a given scale?
**Answer.** Below roughly a few million vectors, almost any option works and the choice is dominated by operational fit (do you already run Postgres with pgvector, do you need a managed service). Past that scale, the real decisions are: approximate vs exact search (exact becomes too slow at scale, so ANN is standard), whether you need metadata filtering *alongside* vector search (pre-filter vs post-filter changes recall characteristics substantially), horizontal scalability/sharding story, and update latency (can you upsert/delete without a full re-index, which matters if your corpus changes frequently). Managed vector DBs (Pinecone, Weaviate, Qdrant Cloud) trade cost and some control for operational simplicity; self-hosted (via a library like FAISS, or pgvector inside your existing Postgres) gives more control and can be cheaper at scale but you own the ops.
**Follow-ups.** Why does metadata filtering interact badly with ANN indexes if done naively? → Post-filtering (retrieve top-k by vector similarity first, then filter by metadata) can return too few or zero results if the top-k happens to be filtered out entirely; pre-filtering (restrict the search space by metadata before the ANN search) avoids that but isn't supported efficiently by every index type, so index choice needs to match your actual filtering needs.
**Page.** [[vector-databases]]

### Q6. Explain HNSW versus IVF — what recall/latency/memory tradeoff is each index actually making?
**Answer.** HNSW builds a multi-layer proximity graph and traverses it greedily from an entry point, giving very good recall at low latency but at high memory cost (the graph structure itself, plus needing the full vectors or a memory-resident approximation) and slower, harder-to-parallelize index builds. IVF partitions the vector space into clusters (via k-means-style centroids) and only searches within the nearest `nprobe` clusters at query time, which is far more memory-efficient and faster to build, but recall depends heavily on `nprobe` and cluster count — too few probed clusters and you miss true neighbors sitting in a nearby-but-unsearched partition. IVF is often paired with product quantization (IVF-PQ) to compress vectors further at some further recall cost, which HNSW doesn't need as urgently since its recall/memory tradeoff is already more favorable per vector.
**Follow-ups.** If memory is your hard constraint and recall can flex slightly, which do you reach for? → IVF (optionally with PQ compression) — HNSW's graph overhead makes it a poor fit when memory, not latency, is the binding constraint.
**Page.** [[ann-algorithms-hnsw-ivf]]

### Q7. Why does pure vector search fail on exact-match or keyword-heavy queries, and how does hybrid search fix it?
**Answer.** Dense embeddings capture semantic similarity, not exact lexical match — a query for a specific product SKU, error code, or proper noun can embed close to semantically-related-but-wrong passages while the passage containing the literal exact string doesn't rank highly, because embedding space doesn't guarantee exact-token proximity. Hybrid search runs a sparse lexical method (BM25/TF-IDF) alongside dense vector search and fuses the two rankings (commonly via reciprocal rank fusion), so exact keyword matches surface even when their semantic embedding isn't the closest, while still getting the semantic recall benefit for paraphrased queries.
**Follow-ups.** What's a concrete query type where hybrid clearly beats pure-vector in a legal/compliance corpus? → A query citing a specific clause number or defined term ("Section 4.2(b)") — BM25 nails the exact string match while a pure embedding may retrieve semantically similar clauses from the wrong section entirely.
**Page.** [[hybrid-search-bm25-vector]]

### Q8. What is query rewriting/expansion, and when do you actually need it?
**Answer.** Query rewriting transforms the user's raw query before retrieval — expanding abbreviations, adding synonyms, decomposing a multi-part question into sub-queries, or rephrasing a conversational follow-up ("what about the second one?") into a self-contained query using conversation history (this is sometimes called query condensation). You need it whenever the gap between how users phrase questions and how the indexed content is phrased/structured is large: short, ambiguous, or context-dependent user queries against long-form formal documents is the classic case where naive retrieval on the raw query underperforms badly.
**Follow-ups.** What's the risk of an LLM-based query rewriter, and how do you bound it? → The rewriter itself can hallucinate a wrong reformulation (inject an assumption the user didn't make) or drift from the user's actual intent — mitigated by keeping the rewrite conservative/templated where possible and always also retrieving on the original query as a fallback signal, not only the rewritten one.
**Page.** [[query-rewriting-and-expansion]]

### Q9. Why rerank after retrieval instead of just retrieving more chunks with a better embedding model?
**Answer.** Bi-encoder embedding models (used for the initial retrieval, since they let you pre-compute and index passage vectors independently of the query) trade off some accuracy for that speed — they score query and passage independently and compare vectors, never letting the query and passage attend to each other. A cross-encoder reranker takes the (query, passage) pair jointly through a model, so it can capture fine-grained interaction the bi-encoder structurally cannot, at the cost of being too slow to run over the whole corpus. The standard pattern is retrieve-then-rerank: use the cheap bi-encoder to get top-k (say 50-100) candidates, then run the expensive cross-encoder only over that shortlist to reorder for precision before feeding the final top-n into the prompt.
**Follow-ups.** Why can't you just make k huge in the initial retrieval and skip reranking? → Context budget is finite and every chunk you add costs prompt tokens/latency and dilutes attention; reranking lets you cheaply search a wide net (large k) at the vector-search stage while still only paying the expensive precision cost, and the LLM's own limited attention span, on the small final set you actually send.
**Page.** [[reranking]]

### Q10. How do you decide what to actually include in the context window once you've retrieved candidates — what does compression buy you here?
**Answer.** More retrieved context isn't free: it costs tokens/latency/money, and models exhibit a "lost in the middle" effect where information in the middle of a long context is used less reliably than information near the start or end, so blindly stuffing in every retrieved chunk can *reduce* answer quality even as it adds more potentially-relevant text. Context assembly involves deduplicating overlapping chunks, ordering the most relevant evidence at the start/end of the context (not buried in the middle), and optionally compressing (extractive summarization of a long chunk down to the sentences actually relevant to the query) so you fit more distinct evidence into a fixed token budget without diluting attention on any one piece.
**Follow-ups.** What's the risk of over-compressing before generation? → An aggressive compression step can strip a caveat or qualifier the generator needed (e.g. "as of 2019" or "in jurisdiction X only"), producing a confidently wrong answer that looks well-grounded because it cites a real but now-mangled passage.
**Page.** [[context-assembly-and-compression]]

### Q11. How do you evaluate a RAG system systematically, separating retrieval quality from generation quality?
**Answer.** You need two separate eval layers, because a good final answer can hide a bad retrieval step (the model got lucky from parametric knowledge) and a good retrieval can be squandered by bad generation. Retrieval metrics: recall@k / precision@k / MRR against a labeled (query, relevant-passage) set, telling you whether the right evidence was even surfaced. Generation/answer metrics: faithfulness (is every claim in the answer actually supported by the retrieved context — usually checked via an LLM-judge or NLI-style entailment model), answer relevance (does it actually address the question), and context relevance (was the retrieved context itself on-topic, independent of what the model did with it). Frameworks like RAGAS operationalize exactly this split so you can localize a failure to retrieval or generation rather than only seeing an end-to-end pass/fail.
**Follow-ups.** How do you build the labeled retrieval eval set without massive manual annotation? → Common approach: generate synthetic (question, source-passage) pairs by having an LLM write questions answerable from a known passage, then validate a sample manually — cheaper than fully manual labeling while still giving ground truth to measure recall@k against.
**Page.** [[rag-evaluation]]

### Q12. Design a multi-hop RAG system for questions that require combining facts scattered across multiple documents.
**Answer.** A single retrieve-then-generate pass fails here because the first retrieval is conditioned only on the original query, which may not surface a document needed for a fact that only becomes relevant after learning an intermediate fact (e.g. "what's the revenue of the company that acquired X" needs first finding who acquired X, then a second retrieval for that company's revenue). The fix is iterative retrieval: decompose the question (explicitly or via an agentic loop) into sub-questions, retrieve for the first sub-question, use that result to formulate the next retrieval query, and repeat until the chain is resolved, then synthesize across all retrieved evidence. This is where advanced RAG patterns and agentic RAG overlap — the decomposition/iteration can be a fixed multi-step pipeline or a model-driven loop deciding when to stop retrieving.
**Follow-ups.** What's the main way this design fails silently in production? → An error or wrong retrieval at an early hop propagates and compounds through every subsequent hop, and because each hop's retrieval looks locally reasonable, the compounding error is hard to detect without a step-by-step trace — you need per-hop logging/evaluation, not just end-to-end answer checking.
**Page.** [[advanced-rag-patterns]]

## Hard

### Q13. When would you reach for GraphRAG over standard vector RAG — what kind of corpus or question actually needs it?
**Answer.** GraphRAG builds an explicit knowledge graph (entities and relationships extracted from the corpus) and retrieves by graph traversal (multi-hop relationship queries, community/cluster summaries) rather than by embedding similarity alone. It earns its complexity when questions are fundamentally relational — "who are all the people connected to X through Y" or "summarize everything related to this entity across the whole corpus" — which vector similarity search structurally can't answer well, because relevance there is about *relationship structure*, not textual similarity to a query. It's a poor fit (unnecessary cost and maintenance) for simple factoid lookup over largely independent documents, where plain vector or hybrid search already works and the graph-construction overhead (entity resolution, relationship extraction, keeping the graph in sync with document updates) buys nothing.
**Follow-ups.** What's the biggest operational cost GraphRAG adds that vector RAG doesn't have? → Keeping the extracted graph consistent with a changing corpus — entity resolution and relationship extraction have to be re-run (or incrementally updated) as documents are added/edited, which is a much heavier pipeline than just re-embedding a new chunk.
**Page.** [[graph-rag]]

### Q14. List the production RAG failure modes you'd actually expect to see, and how each is diagnosed rather than just described.
**Answer.** (1) Retrieval miss — the right passage exists but isn't retrieved (diagnosed via recall@k on a labeled eval set, isolating whether it's an embedding-quality, chunking, or index issue). (2) Retrieved-but-ignored — the right passage is in context but the model's answer doesn't use it (diagnosed by checking whether the passage is present in the assembled context yet the answer contradicts or omits it — points at generation-side faithfulness, or a lost-in-the-middle positioning issue). (3) Hallucinated citation — the answer cites a source that doesn't actually support the claim (diagnosed via an NLI/entailment check between claim and cited passage). (4) Stale index — the corpus changed but the index wasn't refreshed, so correct-looking retrieval returns outdated facts (diagnosed by checking index last-updated timestamps against source document timestamps). Each has a distinct fix — better chunking/embeddings for (1), prompt/compression changes for (2), stricter grounding constraints or a verification pass for (3), and pipeline/freshness SLAs for (4) — so lumping them all into "RAG is hallucinating" without this breakdown wastes debugging effort on the wrong layer.
**Follow-ups.** Which of these four is most often mis-diagnosed as a "model hallucination problem" when it's actually a retrieval problem? → Retrieval miss — teams often try to fix it by prompt-engineering the generator ("don't make things up") when the actual bug is that the correct passage was never in the context in the first place; no prompt fixes that.
**Page.** [[rag-failure-modes]]

### Q15. Your RAG system confidently cites a passage that doesn't actually support the claim in its answer. Walk through root-causing this across the pipeline.
**Answer.** Start by isolating layers rather than guessing: pull the exact assembled context that was sent to the generator for that query and check whether the cited passage is (a) genuinely in the context and (b) actually supports the claim when you read it yourself. If the passage isn't relevant to the claim at all, it's a generation-side grounding failure — the model attributed a claim it produced from parametric memory or over-generalization to the nearest-available citation rather than genuinely deriving the claim from that text; fix with a stricter "only state what's directly supported, and if unsupported say so" instruction plus an automated entailment check as a guardrail before the answer ships. If the passage is topically related but doesn't actually contain the specific fact claimed, it may be a retrieval-precision issue (the true supporting passage exists elsewhere in the corpus but wasn't retrieved, so the model grounded on the closest thing it had) — check recall@k for that query type. If the passage used to genuinely support the claim in a prior version of the source document, it's a staleness issue — check the index's last-refresh time against the document's edit history.
**Follow-ups.** Why is "just add a citation-checking pass" alone not a complete fix? → It catches the symptom (an unsupported citation) after the fact but doesn't address why the model reached for parametric knowledge or a wrong passage in the first place — without also fixing the upstream retrieval/context issue, the checker will keep firing on the same class of query, degrading answer availability rather than fixing answer quality.
**Page.** [[hallucination-and-grounding]]

### Q16. Design the full retrieval stack — chunking, embedding, reranking — for a 500-page legal contract corpus where clauses and cross-references matter.
**Answer.** Chunking: structure-aware, split on clause/section boundaries (never mid-clause) with metadata capturing the section hierarchy (so a chunk knows it's "Section 4.2(b), Indemnification"), since legal text's meaning is heavily bound to its numbered structure and losing that context in a fixed-size split is actively misleading. Embedding: pick a model evaluated on your own labeled (query, clause) pairs rather than a generic benchmark, since legal language (defined terms, formal phrasing) is a distribution shift from typical embedding-model training data; consider a domain-adapted or legal-specific embedding model if generic ones underperform on your eval set. Retrieval: hybrid search is close to mandatory here — clause numbers, defined terms, and party names are exact-match lookups that pure dense retrieval handles poorly, while broader "what does this contract say about liability" queries need the semantic side. Reranking: add a cross-encoder rerank pass given how costly a wrong clause is to miss in a legal context — precision matters more than in a casual Q&A bot, justifying the extra latency.
**Follow-ups.** Why is structure-preserving metadata (section/clause hierarchy) arguably more valuable here than the choice of embedding model itself? → Because in legal text, a correct answer often needs to cite *which* clause and how it relates to others (e.g. an exception carved out three sections later) — losing that structural context at chunking time is unrecoverable downstream no matter how good the embedding or reranker is; you can't rerank your way back to context that was discarded at ingestion.
**Page.** [[chunking-strategies]]

### Q17. Compare agentic RAG (retrieval as a model-invoked tool) against a fixed retrieve-then-generate pipeline, and say when the added complexity is actually worth it.
**Answer.** Fixed RAG is simpler, cheaper, and has bounded, predictable latency (one retrieval call, one generation call) — the right default for straightforward Q&A over a single corpus where one retrieval pass is usually sufficient. Agentic RAG earns its cost when queries genuinely vary in how much/what kind of retrieval they need: some questions need zero retrieval (answerable from general knowledge or the conversation itself), some need one lookup, some need iterative multi-hop retrieval or retrieval from multiple distinct sources with the model deciding which — a fixed pipeline either over-retrieves for the simple cases (wasted cost/latency, more irrelevant context diluting the answer) or under-retrieves for the complex ones (multi-hop questions fail outright). The complexity is worth paying for when query variety is high enough that a single fixed retrieval policy measurably under- or over-serves a significant fraction of real queries — verify this with actual query logs before adding agentic complexity on assumption alone.
**Follow-ups.** What's a cheap intermediate option between fully fixed and fully agentic retrieval? → A single upfront routing/classification step (does this query need retrieval at all, and from which source) followed by one fixed retrieval — captures most of the benefit of adaptive retrieval without the unbounded, multi-round-trip cost of a full agentic loop.
**Page.** [[agentic-rag]]

### Q18. You're stacking hybrid search, reranking, and multi-hop retrieval, but you're under a strict latency SLA. What would you cut first, and why?
**Answer.** Cut multi-hop iteration first — it's the only one of the three whose cost is *unbounded and sequential* (each hop is a full round trip that depends on the previous one finishing, so latency multiplies with hop count), whereas hybrid search and reranking are single-pass additions with a fixed, predictable latency cost each. Before cutting it entirely, first check whether most of your actual query traffic even needs multi-hop (if it's a small minority of queries, route only those through the multi-hop path and keep single-hop hybrid+rerank as the fast default for everything else, rather than dropping the capability altogether). If you must cut further, reranking is the next lever — reduce the candidate set size fed into the cross-encoder (rerank fewer, better pre-filtered candidates) rather than dropping reranking outright, since it's usually the single biggest precision lever per unit of added latency.
**Follow-ups.** Why is it wrong to think of these three techniques as independent latency costs you can just sum and cut from? → Because their value is often complementary rather than additive-in-isolation — e.g. hybrid search's main benefit (catching exact-match queries dense search misses) is partially wasted if you then cut reranking, since a noisier candidate set needs reranking more, not less, to actually pay off; cuts should be evaluated on end-to-end answer quality per query type, not per-component in isolation.
